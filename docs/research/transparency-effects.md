# Does grocery price transparency help or hurt shoppers?

Research ticket [#5](https://github.com/exilonX/cart-compare/issues/5) on the Grocery Basket Index map ([#1](https://github.com/exilonX/cart-compare/issues/1)). Desk research, 2026-10-21. Terms follow the project glossary (`CONTEXT.md`): Basket, Chain, Shelf price, Loyalty price, Comparable, Social value, Demand signal.

**Evidence tiers used below.** **T1** = causal academic study (identification strategy named). **T1-s** = academic structural model or descriptive study: rigorous, but not a clean causal estimate of a policy. **T2** = regulator evaluation, decision or market study. **T3** = usage statistics. **T4** = media report or press release. **Theory** = formal economic model.

**Two kinds of transparency.** *Consumer-facing* transparency puts prices in front of shoppers, through a portal, app, media survey or index. *Competitor-facing* transparency gives Chains extra, faster or more detailed information about each other's prices: price-scanning arrangements, data-sharing platforms, or firm-specific transaction prices that buyers negotiate in private. Almost every consumer-facing tool can also be read by competitors. The question that matters is what the tool tells Chains that they did not already know.

## Answer

- **The causal evidence is mixed, and the direction depends on design and market conditions.** The one causal study of a grocery disclosure rule (Israel, 2015) found prices fell 4–5% ([Ater & Rigbi 2023](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mic.20200337), T1, difference-in-differences). Germany's fuel price monitor lowered prices by roughly 1–3% ([Montag, Sagimuldina & Winter 2023](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/105662/1/415.pdf), T1). Chile's fuel disclosure raised margins about 9% on average ([Luco 2019](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mic.20170110), T1). Denmark's publication of concrete prices was followed by price rises of 15–20% ([Albæk, Møllgaard & Overgaard 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-6451.00057), T1, before/after).
- **The harms come from information that helps sellers more than buyers.** Each documented price increase involved firm-identified, frequent or granular data reaching sellers who did not already have it (the Danish secret discounts, the 15-minute rival feed in Melbourne, reciprocal price-scanning in Norway's grocery Chains), or a market where few buyers use the data (low-search regions of Chile). Norway fined its three grocery groups NOK 4.9 billion in 2024 for mutual price-scanning access that "increased price transparency between the chains" while consumers did not become better informed ([Konkurransetilsynet 2024](https://konkurransetilsynet.no/coop-norgesgruppen-and-rema-fined-4-9-billion-nok/?lang=en), T2).
- **Where transparency helped, it reached poorly informed buyers, and mostly through the media rather than comparison sites.** In Israel only 4% of consumers had used a comparison site in a year (T3). Prices fell because discount Chains ran adverts quoting media price surveys, which had become cheap to produce ([Ater & Rigbi 2018 WP](https://falk.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/falkheb/files/paper_18-04a.pdf)). In Germany prices fell more for petrol, whose buyers were less informed beforehand, than for diesel ([Montag et al. 2023](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/105662/1/415.pdf)).
- **Competition authorities see low marginal coordination risk from consumer-facing grocery price publication, because Chains already monitor each other closely.** The ACCC (2025) recommended mandatory online prices and a live price API for Coles and Woolworths. It found that publishing prices "is unlikely to materially increase collusion risks", citing New Zealand's Commerce Commission (2022) ([ACCC final report, Box 3.5, p. 114](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/supermarkets-inquiry_1.pdf), T2). Canada's Competition Bureau (2023) backed unit pricing and price comparison tools and did not discuss coordination risk ([Competition Bureau 2023](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/how-we-foster-competition/education-and-outreach/canada-needs-more-grocery-competition), T2). EU guidelines treat genuinely public, committed and aggregated prices as low-risk. The risk rises with frequency, granularity, firm identification, future intentions, and information made transparent "for competitors rather than for consumers" ([EU Horizontal Guidelines 2023, ch. 6](https://competition-policy.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2023-07/2023_revised_horizontal_guidelines_en.pdf), T2).
- **There is no independent causal evaluation of Hungary's Árfigyelő or Croatia's price-list rules yet.** The only Árfigyelő estimate is a synthetic-control study by Makronóm Intézet, published through the competition authority's press release: food inflation 1.11 pp lower by February 2024 ([GVH, 4 Sep 2024](https://www.gvh.hu/en/press_room/press_releases/press-releases-2024/hungarian-families-saved-nearly-huf-20-billion-in-three-months-thanks-to-the-online-price-monitor), T2/T4). The method is unpublished, and the period overlaps with the end of price caps and higher mandatory discounts in July–August 2023. For Croatia's rules (in force since 15 May 2025) I found only descriptive analyses, and price caps confound the period.
- **A weekly, aggregated, Shelf-price Basket index sits at the low-risk end of every dimension that predicts harm**: low frequency, already-public prices, committed rather than announced prices, aggregated totals, and many Chains of different types. The design choices that would push it towards harm are publishing item-level data at higher frequency, selling Chains early or premium data feeds, giving access asymmetrically, or forecasting price moves. Choices that push it towards help are reaching shoppers through the press, covering the people least likely to compare prices, setting clear rules for "cheapest Chain" claims, and showing Shelf and Loyalty prices side by side.
- **Harms to name openly**: (a) gains may skip the least-informed shoppers, who in Chile were in lower-income, low-search areas; (b) exposing price differences between stores can push Chains to uniform pricing (Israel), which can raise prices in some areas; (c) Chains may game a known snapshot day; (d) predictable price timing can raise margins: Germany's 2026 rule allowing price increases only at noon raised petrol margins by 5–6 c/l ([Jung et al. 2026](https://www.zew.de/fileadmin/FTP/gutachten/ZEW-DICE-Analysis-Predictable-Prices-Higher-Margins-April2026.pdf), T1, interrupted time series).

### Israel: the only causal study of a grocery rule (T1)

- **The rule.** Israel's Food Act passed in March 2014. From 20 May 2015 each supermarket Chain had to upload prices and promotions for every product in every store. Files were refreshed daily, and within an hour of any price change ([Ater & Rigbi 2018 WP, pp. 7–8](https://falk.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/falkheb/files/paper_18-04a.pdf)). Before the rule took effect, Chain CEOs warned it would "facilitate collusion" (same source, p. 8).
- **Identification.** The authors hired a survey firm to record store prices in the year between the law passing and taking effect. They compared these items with four control groups whose transparency did not change: the same products in the Chains' online shops, the consumer council basket, drugstores, and small grocers, all of which were exempt. Results were consistent across all four ([pp. 3–4](https://falk.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/falkheb/files/paper_18-04a.pdf)). The paper was published as [Ater & Rigbi, AEJ: Microeconomics 15(1), 2023](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mic.20200337).
- **Effects.** Prices fell 4–5% more than in the control groups, mostly at premium Chains and less where local competition was already strong. Branded products fell more than comparable private-label ones. Within-Chain price dispersion dropped quickly as Chains moved to uniform national pricing. Price levels changed significantly only from early 2016, months after the rule ([pp. 4–5, 23](https://falk.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/falkheb/files/paper_18-04a.pdf)). A back-of-envelope estimate puts household savings at about US$27 a month, about 1.5% of the 2015 median wage (p. 30).
- **Mechanism.** Hard-discount Chains raised spending on adverts citing *media price surveys*, which transparency made cheap to produce. They used these adverts most when their prices were lowest. This matches the Robert and Stahl (1993) model of informative advertising. Consumers rarely used the comparison sites themselves (pp. 4–5, 21).
- **Usage (T3).** In a consumer-organisation survey, 4% of respondents had used a comparison site in the previous year. Two sites averaged 21,414 and 16,992 monthly unique visitors (Oct 2015–Jul 2016), together about 2% of households. A government TV campaign and prize competition did not lift traffic. At least two of these Comparables made their money selling business-intelligence services to retailers and suppliers ([pp. 21–22](https://falk.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/falkheb/files/paper_18-04a.pdf)).
- **Caveats.** The authors stress the short window and that "results … may change in the long run" (p. 30). They attribute uniform pricing to fairness concerns, not coordination (p. 22). Uniform pricing ended the pattern of higher prices in poorer, rural areas reported in Israeli media. It can, in principle, also remove local discounts.

### Denmark, ready-mixed concrete (T1, early design)

- In 1993 the Danish competition authority began publishing firm-specific *transaction* prices for two grades of ready-mixed concrete in three regions. Average prices of the reported grades rose 15–20% within a year. A business upturn and capacity constraints had little explanatory power, so the authors conclude publication let firms "reduce the intensity of oligopoly price competition" ([Albæk, Møllgaard & Overgaard 1997, JIE 45(4)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jindec/v45y1997i4p429-443.html)). Montag and co-authors note the policy was later repealed ([ProMarket 2024](https://www.promarket.org/2024/07/23/when-does-mandatory-price-disclosure-lower-prices/)).
- **Why it matters for groceries.** The buyers were construction firms negotiating private discounts, and they already knew their own quotes. Publication mainly told *sellers* what rivals were charging, which is competitor-facing transparency by another route. Grocery Shelf prices are already public and committed, so this exact channel is weaker for a Basket index.

### Chile, fuel price disclosure (T1)

- Chile introduced online fuel price disclosure region by region. Using the staggered rollout, Luco finds margins rose by about 9% on average. The increase was smallest, and margins sometimes fell, in high-search areas, which also have higher incomes. He concludes disclosure can have "important distributional effects" ([Luco 2019, AEJ: Micro 11(2)](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mic.20170110)).
- **Lesson.** When firms read the data more than consumers do, the net effect can be higher prices. The harm falls on those least likely to search, who are the Basket index's priority users (families on a budget, pensioners).

### Germany, MTS-K and the 2026 timing rule (T1, T1-s, T2)

- **Design.** Since late 2013 every public fuel station reports each price change for E5, E10 and diesel to the Bundeskartellamt's transparency unit within five minutes. The unit runs no public website of its own. It forwards data to licensed consumer information services (43 as of June 2026), and it collects no volume data ([Kahl & Wein, Sept 2026, pp. 3, 10](https://www.leuphana.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Forschungseinrichtungen/ifvwl/WorkingPapers/wp_444_Upload.pdf)).
- **Price effect.** In a DiD with French stations as controls, mandatory disclosure cut petrol prices by about 2.7% and diesel by about 1.8%. The effect settled at 1–2% after about five months, and follow-on local radio price reports strengthened it ([Montag, Sagimuldina & Winter 2023 WP](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/105662/1/415.pdf), published 2026 in [JPE Microeconomics 4(1)](https://doi.org/10.1086/734145)). In cents, the cuts were 4–6 c/l for petrol and 2–4 c/l for diesel. Local monopolists cut least, and integrated oligopoly brands cut most. Dispersion fell because average prices dropped more than minimum prices, so already-informed buyers gained less. The authors' preconditions are that consumers lack prior information, producers are already well informed, and there are three or more competitors ([ProMarket 2024](https://www.promarket.org/2024/07/23/when-does-mandatory-price-disclosure-lower-prices/)). A synthetic-DiD study over 2005–2018 finds a long-run fall of 2–3 c/l in Germany and no significant change in France ([Bernhardt, Breiderhoff & Dewenter 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10842-024-00438-1)). Early evaluations using national averages to 2015 were mixed ([Kahl & Wein 2026, p. 3](https://www.leuphana.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Forschungseinrichtungen/ifvwl/WorkingPapers/wp_444_Upload.pdf)).
- **How much to show (T1-s).** A structural model of German consumer search finds welfare is maximised when apps list only the cheapest 20% of stations, cutting consumer spending by 1.5% compared with listing all stations ([Martin 2024, RAND J. Econ. 55(4)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/randje/v55y2024i4p573-602.html)). Austria's scheme publishes only the cheaper half of stations ([Jung et al. 2026, p. 1](https://www.zew.de/fileadmin/FTP/gutachten/ZEW-DICE-Analysis-Predictable-Prices-Higher-Margins-April2026.pdf)).
- **Algorithms.** After pricing software spread from 2017, adoption raised margins only in non-monopoly markets, and in tight oligopolies only when rivals adopted too ([Assad, Clark, Ershov & Xu 2024, JPE 132(3)](https://doi.org/10.1086/726906), as summarised by [Kahl & Wein 2026, pp. 3–4](https://www.leuphana.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Forschungseinrichtungen/ifvwl/WorkingPapers/wp_444_Upload.pdf)). Real-time public price feeds are an input to such software.
- **Twelve-year review (T1-s, descriptive).** Kahl & Wein find more daily price cycles and wider intraday spreads, with weekday and holiday price differences gone. They conclude there is "no evidence that increased market transparency has harmed competition". They also note that cycles are not proof that prices fell to the competitive floor, and that the refinery and wholesale stages remain collusion-prone ([pp. 22–23](https://www.leuphana.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Forschungseinrichtungen/ifvwl/WorkingPapers/wp_444_Upload.pdf)).
- **Usage (T3).** In the Bundeskartellamt's 2018 evaluation (Bundestag Drucksache 19/3693), about three-quarters of drivers had heard of the apps, one-third had used one, and one-quarter typically used them. Services reported about one million unique users a month ([Kahl & Wein 2026, p. 11](https://www.leuphana.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Forschungseinrichtungen/ifvwl/WorkingPapers/wp_444_Upload.pdf)).
- **2026 timing rule.** From 1 April 2026 stations may raise prices only once a day, at noon. An interrupted-time-series study with no control group finds petrol margins up 5–6 c/l and no diesel effect, strongest right after the change. The authors conclude the rule "was successful in increasing price transparency but failed to reduce price levels" ([Jung, Schildknecht, Gregor & Haucap, ZEW/DICE, April 2026](https://www.zew.de/fileadmin/FTP/gutachten/ZEW-DICE-Analysis-Predictable-Prices-Higher-Margins-April2026.pdf)).

### Australia, fuel: Perth and Melbourne (T1-s, T1)

- **Perth.** Using 15 years of station-level prices for one city, Byrne & de Roos document a three-year transition in which "dominant firms use price leadership and price experiments" to create focal points that "soften price competition, and enhance retail margins" ([Byrne & de Roos 2019, AER 109(2)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v109y2019i2p591-619.html)). The market is Perth, 2001 onward. The data cover every station's price, so rivals could see each other's moves, and leaders announced moves "a day ahead". As far as I know these are the prices Western Australia's FuelWatch scheme makes public, but I did not re-verify that for this ticket. A conference discussant summarised the margin rise as "up to 75%" ([FTC discussion slides, 2016](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/945353/rahman_learning_0.pdf)).
- **Melbourne, Informed Sources.** Five retailers shared complete station-level prices every 15–30 minutes through a subscriber-only platform that consumers could not see. The ACCC alleged it worked as "a near real-time communication device". Under the December 2015 settlement, the platform had to offer its data to third parties, including consumer apps, and Coles left in April 2016. No new consumer app entered. After Coles left, margins rose by 5.9 c/l for Coles and 3.4 c/l for rivals, about 50%, and rivals' profits rose 38–77%, or about A$33m a year. Rivals' informational advantage gave the less-informed firm "price commitment" ([Byrne, de Roos, Lewis, Marx & Wu 2023 WP, pp. 1–3, 6–9](https://people.duke.edu/~marx/bio/papers/AsymInfoSharing.pdf); published in [JPE 133(7), 2025](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1604509)). Disclosure: Byrne and Marx were retained by the ACCC in the case, and de Roos by 7-Eleven.
- **Lessons.** Competitor-facing feeds are where coordination happens. Fixing them is not simple: an asymmetric fix can raise prices. Opening a data feed to apps does not create consumer use on its own.

### Norway, grocery price scanning (T2)

- From January 2011 until inspections in April 2018, Norgesgruppen, Coop and Rema let each other's price hunters scan prices in their stores with hand scanners. Over time the Chains received competitor price updates "several times a day". Seized documents showed the data was used "to test whether competitors would participate in price increases". In 2007 ACNielsen had stopped weekly price reports to the Chains after the authority warned they could be illegal. The August 2024 decision fined the Chains NOK 4.9 billion: Coop 1.3, Norgesgruppen 2.3, Rema 1.3. It found the arrangement "increased price transparency between the chains" without making consumers "better informed about prices" ([Konkurransetilsynet, 21 Aug 2024](https://konkurransetilsynet.no/coop-norgesgruppen-and-rema-fined-4-9-billion-nok/?lang=en)). The authority dropped the object theory in January 2024 and proceeded on effects. The Competition Appeals Board upheld the finding on 20 August 2025 ([Concurrences](https://www.concurrences.com/en/bulletin/news-issues/august-2025/the-norwegian-competition-appeals-board-upholds-fines-on-3-grocery-retailers)). I did not check whether the case has gone to court since.
- **Lesson.** This is the clearest grocery example of transparency that informs competitors rather than shoppers. The harm came from high frequency, reciprocal access and fast matching, which a weekly public index does not provide.

### Hungary, Árfigyelő (T2/T4; no independent causal study)

- **Design.** The competition authority (GVH) launched the portal with the government on 1 July 2023, covering 62 food categories with daily prices from six national Chains ([GVH, 10 Nov 2023](https://www.gvh.hu/en/press_room/press_releases/press-releases-2023/gvh-the-price-monitoring-database-in-hand-with-government-measures-has-helped-to-achieve-single-digit-inflation)). By March 2026 it covered more than 5,000 products in 140 categories, across six grocery and three drugstore Chains and 1,817 stores ([Trademagazin, 10 Mar 2026](https://trademagazin.hu/en/gvh-az-arfigyelo-is-kordaban-tartja-az-elelmiszerarakat/)).
- **Claimed effects.** In November 2023 the GVH reported that average prices had fallen in 50 of 62 categories by 5.5% on average since launch. It credited the database "together with government measures" and did not isolate its effect ([GVH, 10 Nov 2023](https://www.gvh.hu/en/press_room/press_releases/press-releases-2023/gvh-the-price-monitoring-database-in-hand-with-government-measures-has-helped-to-achieve-single-digit-inflation)). In September 2024 the GVH released a synthetic-control estimate by the Makronóm Institute: food inflation 1.11 pp lower by February 2024 and 1.45 pp lower on average over December 2023–February 2024, with significant effects in 14 of 21 subcategories, HUF 19.4 billion saved by households over those three months, and about 0.2 pp off headline inflation. The release gives no donor pool, specification or confidence intervals ([GVH, 4 Sep 2024](https://www.gvh.hu/en/press_room/press_releases/press-releases-2024/hungarian-families-saved-nearly-huf-20-billion-in-three-months-thanks-to-the-online-price-monitor)). Press coverage did not link a full study ([Pénzcentrum, 4 Sep 2024](https://www.penzcentrum.hu/vasarlas/20240904/eddig-bejott-a-nagy-talalmany-azt-mondjak-temerdek-penzt-sporoltak-a-magyarok-ezzel-az-egy-honlappal-1155968/amp)). The GVH has also stated that retailers' gross margins on dairy fell by about 10 pp, with no method given ([Trademagazin, 2026](https://trademagazin.hu/en/gvh-az-arfigyelo-is-kordaban-tartja-az-elelmiszerarakat/)).
- **Confounders.** Food price caps ended on 31 July 2023 ([Eurofound](https://static.eurofound.europa.eu/covid19db/cases/HU-2022-6_3084.html)). The mandatory discount rule rose from 10% to 15% on 1 August 2023 ([Just Food, 23 Jun 2023](https://www.just-food.com/news/hungary-to-scrap-food-price-cap-scheme-just-food/)). A 10% retail margin cap on about 30 food groups started in mid-March 2025 ([Just Food, 12 Mar 2025](https://www.just-food.com/news/hungary-to-cap-profit-margins-on-food-items)). Any estimate for 2023–2026 has to separate the portal from these measures and from falling producer prices.
- **Use in advertising (T2).** The GVH investigated Lidl's "champion of low prices" slogan, which was built on Árfigyelő basket averages. In November 2025 it closed the case with commitments. Lidl will cite the source, and will claim to be cheapest in a month only if the second-cheapest Chain's basket is at least 2.5% dearer and Lidl was cheapest on more days ([Economx, 13 Nov 2025](https://www.economx.hu/magyar-gazdasag/2025/11/13/gvh-versenyhivatal-eljaras-lidl-arak-819279/)). This is the same advertising channel that drove the Israeli price falls, with a substantiation rule attached.

### Croatia, price-list rules (no evaluation found)

- **Design.** Government decision NN 75/2025 (2 May 2025), in force from 15 May 2025, requires supermarkets, hypermarkets, discount stores and cash-and-carry outlets to publish daily store price lists online as machine-readable CSV or XML. It also requires an additional reference price to be displayed in store ([zakon.hr](https://www.zakon.hr/c/podzakonski-propis/540799/nn-75-2025-%282.5.2025.%29%2C-odluka-o-objavi-cjenika-i-isticanju-dodatne-cijene-kao-mjera-izravne-kontrole-cijena-u-trgovini-na); [TEB](https://teb.hr/novosti/2025/objava-cjenika-i-isticanje-dodatne-cijene/)). Amendments in NN 101/2026 extend the duties to all sellers of goods and services from 17 November 2026 ([TEB](https://teb.hr/novosti/2026/dodatna-cijena-i-objava-cjenika-nova-pravila-od-1102026-odgodeno-na-17112026/)).
- **Evidence.** I found no HNB, Institute of Economics or academic study of the rule's effect on prices. The Institute of Economics, Zagreb (Tkalec & Žilić) used the first files, 8 million price entries for 116,000 products in 776 stores of 11 Chains, to describe dispersion. Zadar county averaged 7.9% above the cheapest county, and one kefir had 11 different prices nationwide ([Croatia Week, 27 May 2025](https://www.croatiaweek.com/revealed-the-15-most-expensive-places-in-croatia-to-shop/), T4). The period is confounded by price caps on 70 products, announced January 2025 ([Croatia Week](https://www.croatiaweek.com/croatian-government-imposes-price-caps-on-70-essential-goods/)), and widened to 100 products from 1 December 2025 ([ESM](https://www.esmmagazine.com/retail/croatia-expands-price-caps-to-100-essential-products-300877)).
- **Regulator view (T2).** In a study covering September 2024 to February 2025, before the rule, the Croatian Competition Agency found that the six largest Chains say they set prices independently "while simultaneously observing the behaviour of competing retailers using publicly available data". It found insufficient indications to open a case ([AZTN](https://www.aztn.hr/en/provedeno-ispitivanje-nacina-odredivanja-maloprodajnih-cijena-10-prehrambenih-proizvoda-u-trgovini-na-malo-te-ispitivanje-najave-dobavljaca-trgovcima-na-malo-o-povecanju-cijena/)).

### Competition authorities (T2)

- **ACCC, Supermarkets Inquiry final report (February 2025, released 21 March 2025).**
  - **Recommendation 2.** All supermarkets should publish in-store prices. ALDI, Coles and Woolworths should also publish prices online. Coles and Woolworths should provide APIs with dynamic prices, including unit prices, to third parties "such as online price comparison tools and researchers". The ACCC judged this burden reasonable for very large Chains ([pp. 24, 111–114](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/supermarkets-inquiry_1.pdf)).
  - **Box 3.5.** Requiring retailers to publish prices online "is unlikely to materially increase collusion risks". The box quotes the New Zealand Commerce Commission's 2022 grocery study: the additional coordination risk is "likely to be low" because "a high degree of price monitoring is already undertaken by firms". ALDI, Coles, Metcash and Woolworths each told hearings that they closely monitor each other's prices, including by web scraping ([pp. 110, 114](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/supermarkets-inquiry_1.pdf)).
  - **Other points.** The report flags that comparison sites funded by Chains raise independence concerns, and suggests funding disclosure or government grants. It recalls that the government's GROCERYchoice site, launched after the 2008 inquiry, was discontinued in 2009 ([pp. 110–111](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/supermarkets-inquiry_1.pdf)). Personalised loyalty offers and bonus points "are likely to reduce price transparency", and comparison tools are "unlikely to be able to take into account" them ([pp. 228, 241](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/supermarkets-inquiry_1.pdf)). The report also cites a UK CMA survey in which 72% agreed that lower loyalty prices make them feel they must join loyalty schemes ([p. 231](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/supermarkets-inquiry_1.pdf)).
- **Canada, Competition Bureau, "Canada Needs More Grocery Competition" (27 June 2023).** The Bureau recommends harmonised, accessible unit pricing. It says price comparison tools "have the potential to empower consumers". Flyer-based tools such as Flipp compare only promoted items, so "if all grocers provided their data to these services" comparison would be easier, though "further study would be required". The report does not discuss coordination risk from transparency. It notes that parallel pricing without agreement is not illegal ([Competition Bureau 2023](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/how-we-foster-competition/education-and-outreach/canada-needs-more-grocery-competition)).
- **European Union, Horizontal Guidelines (OJ C 259, 21.7.2023), chapter 6.** The guidelines set out the following ([DG COMP PDF](https://competition-policy.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2023-07/2023_revised_horizontal_guidelines_en.pdf)):
  - Information put in the public domain for legitimate reasons and "readily accessible (in terms of access costs) to all competitors and customers" is usually not commercially sensitive. Information is not public if collection costs deter others (s. 6.2.3.1).
  - Exchanging already-public data can still restrict competition if it is more granular, more frequent, or carries comments that signal desired joint action (s. 6.2.3.1).
  - Aggregated data across products is less likely to restrict competition, except in very tight, stable oligopolies (s. 6.2.3.2).
  - Current information is riskier "especially if the exchange serves to artificially increase transparency for competitors rather than for consumers" (s. 6.2.3.3).
  - Frequent, and especially real-time automated, exchange raises risk (s. 6.2.4.3).
  - Public announcements of *future pricing intentions*, unlike committed price changes, "generally do not create benefits for consumers and they may facilitate collusion" (s. 6.2.4.1).
  - Publishing price-comparison data can help consumers reduce search costs (s. 6.3).
- **Theory.** In standard models transparency on the consumer side makes tacit collusion harder to sustain, because undercutting wins more customers ([Schultz 2005, EER 49(2)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eecrev/v49y2005i2p279-297.html)). Transparency on the producer side makes deviations easier to detect and punish. In a Varian-type model, the more consumers are uninformed beforehand, the larger the price fall from new information ([Montag et al. 2023](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/105662/1/415.pdf)).

## Conditions for help vs harm

| Condition | Pushes towards help | Pushes towards harm | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What sellers already know | Chains already monitor each other, so the tool adds little for them | The tool gives sellers information they lacked (secret discounts, faster or finer rival data) | Denmark; Norway; ACCC/NZCC Box 3.5; Montag et al. |
| Who uses it | Poorly informed shoppers start comparing, directly or through media | Few shoppers use it while firms all do; low-search areas | Israel; Chile; Montag et al. |
| Channel to shoppers | Press coverage and Chains' price adverts citing a trusted survey | A portal or API that nobody visits | Israel (≈2% reach of sites, media mechanism); Informed Sources (no apps entered) |
| Frequency and timing | Low frequency; prices already committed | Real-time feeds; predictable timing of price moves; announced future prices | Norway; Informed Sources; Germany 2026; EU HG 6.2.4 |
| Granularity and identity | Aggregated across many differentiated products | Firm-, store- and SKU-level, high frequency, in a homogeneous product | EU HG 6.2.3.2; Denmark |
| Market structure | Three or more competitors; mix of discount and premium | Tight, stable oligopoly with a homogeneous product (fuel, concrete) | Montag et al.; Ater & Rigbi; EU HG |
| Access | Same open access for everyone | Asymmetric access, or premium/early feeds sold to Chains | Byrne et al. 2025; Israeli Comparables selling BI to retailers |
| Show all vs show best | Highlighting the cheapest strengthens undercutting | Showing the full distribution also serves firms | Martin 2024; Austria shows cheaper half |

### What this means for a weekly Basket index

The current design in the Project Overview: weekly snapshot, 8 Chains, Basket totals and per-item history, Shelf and Loyalty prices, published for shoppers and press.

- **Aggregation level.** Basket totals per Chain are aggregated across about 20–30 differentiated items, which is the low-risk form under EU guidance. Per-item price history adds granularity. The marginal information it gives Chains is small, because the same Shelf prices are already on Chains' websites and Chains already scrape each other (as documented in Australia and Croatia). The project's own rule to "publish aggregated basket totals and trends, not a mirror of each retailer's catalogue" points in the low-risk direction.
- **Delay and frequency.** A weekly snapshot is slower than the daily or real-time feeds behind every documented coordination case. Adding a lag would cost shoppers more than it reduces coordination risk, because Chains already have real-time data. A delay matters only if the index publishes something Chains do not have. One related risk is gaming: if the snapshot day and time are known, a Chain could price for the snapshot. This is an accuracy and truthfulness problem rather than collusion.
- **Granularity by place.** Store-level pricing is out of MVP scope. Publishing city-level differences could push Chains towards uniform pricing, as in Israel. That could help the regions that pay more and hurt those that currently get local discounts.
- **Shelf vs Loyalty price.** The Shelf price is the committed, public price, the low-risk category under EU guidance. Showing the Loyalty price next to it makes the cost of not joining visible, which serves shoppers who stay out of loyalty schemes. The ACCC flags that personalised loyalty offers reduce comparability. It also cites a CMA survey in which 72% of UK shoppers felt lower loyalty prices pushed them to join schemes. Personalised offers differ by account, are not Comparable across shoppers, and should stay out.
- **Access.** Open data on equal terms keeps the information symmetric. Selling Chains earlier, finer or premium feeds would turn a consumer tool into competitor intelligence, which is where the evidence locates harm.
- **Claims and commentary.** Expect Chains to advertise rankings, as in Israel and with Lidl in Hungary. Publishing ties and margins of error, and a "cheapest" threshold like the GVH's 2.5% rule, keeps claims accurate. Neutral, backward-looking commentary avoids forecasting or signalling price moves, which EU guidance links to collusion.

## Gaps and uncertainties

- **No causal study of a published grocery *basket index*.** All causal grocery evidence concerns full price disclosure (Israel). The closest analogue to the Basket index is the media price surveys that drove the Israeli effect, which were studied as a mechanism, not evaluated alone. Australia's GROCERYchoice (2008–09) closed without an evaluation that I found.
- **Hungary and Croatia lack independent evaluations.** The Árfigyelő estimate comes from a study released by the authority that runs the portal, with the method unpublished and strong confounders. Croatia has only descriptive work, and price caps run in parallel.
- **Long-run effects are unknown.** The Israeli estimates cover about one year after the rule. Fuel results drift over time: Montag et al. find effects shrink, Perth shows a coordination equilibrium emerging over three years, and Kahl & Wein note no estimate covers 2022–2026.
- **External validity of fuel.** Most causal studies are of fuel, a homogeneous product with few sellers per local market and visible pole prices. Groceries involve thousands of SKUs, promotions and private label, which make coordination on a whole basket harder. They also make like-for-like comparison harder (the ACCC notes this, p. 113).
- **Loyalty pricing.** I found no causal evidence on the effect of *publishing* Loyalty prices.
- **Sources not re-read first-hand.** The OECD roundtables on price transparency and information exchange, the Bundeskartellamt's 2018 MTS-K evaluation (used via Kahl & Wein), the NZCC 2022 study (used via the ACCC quote), and the published versions of several working papers. Figures cite the version read. The web-search budget for this session ran out before the OECD material could be located.
- **Romania.** This ticket did not examine whether Monitorul Prețurilor has been evaluated, or what market shares Romanian Chains hold. Market concentration changes how much aggregated data could help coordination.

## Questions this raises

1. **Publication policy (aggregation and frequency).** Should item-level price history be public, or only Basket totals and trends? At what cadence? This needs a decision and could be a grilling ticket.
2. **Snapshot method.** Should collection time be fixed or randomised, and disclosed or not, to stop Chains pricing for the snapshot while keeping the method verifiable?
3. **Claims policy.** What rules apply when Chains or media cite the index ("cheapest Chain this week")? Options include tie thresholds, minimum gaps (as in the GVH/Lidl commitments) and attribution requirements.
4. **Data access.** Open data on equal terms, or totals only? Should there be a written commitment never to sell early, premium or granular feeds to Chains or suppliers?
5. **Shelf vs Loyalty presentation.** How should the Loyalty price be shown so the cost of not joining is visible, and how should personalised offers be excluded?
6. **Media partnership as the main channel.** The Israeli evidence makes newsrooms, not site traffic, the primary route to price effects. This bears on the Partnerships item.
7. **Measuring Social value.** Measuring any price effect later needs a pre-launch baseline and a comparison group, such as non-covered products, another city, or a neighbouring country. This affects the build effort, which is out of scope here.
8. **Competition Council.** Has the Consiliul Concurenței evaluated Monitorul Prețurilor? What is its view of a civic index? These belong in the lawyer and partnership consults.
9. **Campaigning for a Croatia-style duty.** The grocery evidence favours consumer-facing mandatory disclosure (Israel). Croatia's rule is unevaluated. The project could offer to evaluate it rather than assume it works.
