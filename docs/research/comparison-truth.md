# Can a comparison between regions and countries tell the truth about cost of living?

Research ticket [#6](https://github.com/exilonX/cart-compare/issues/6) on map [#1](https://github.com/exilonX/cart-compare/issues/1). Desk research, written 2026-10-21. Terms (Basket, Chain, Shelf price, Loyalty price, Comparable) follow the glossary in `CONTEXT.md`.

**Evidence tiers used below**

- **[T1]** academic empirical study.
- **[T2]** regulator, statistical office or Commission evaluation, including official methodology.
- **[T3]** usage statistics.
- **[T4]** media report or anecdote.
- **[Law]** legal text.
- **[Sec]** secondary professional summary (for example a tax firm's country guide).

All figures carry the date of the data and of the release. Where a primary page could not be read, the claim is attributed to the secondary source that reported it.

## Answer

- **A grocery Basket cannot measure "cost of living", but it can truthfully measure grocery affordability.** Food and non-alcoholic drinks are 22.7% of household consumption in Romania but 10.9% in Germany (Eurostat, 2025). Rent, energy and transport are left out entirely. The index should claim "what this Basket costs, and how many minutes of work it takes", never "cost of living". [T2]
- **"We pay more than Germans" is false for food prices as a whole, true for some categories, and true in work time.** Eurostat's 2025 price levels (EU = 100) put Romanian food and non-alcoholic drinks at 79.8 against Germany's 102.4. Romania is still at or above German levels for dairy and eggs (104.7 vs 96.1), sugar and confectionery (94.5 vs 93.5) and ready-made food (92.5 vs 92.4). It is far cheaper for meat, fruit, vegetables and cereals. Against net income, the same food takes about **2.0 to 2.8 times as much work** in Romania as in Germany, depending on which income measure is used. [T2]
- **The planned "International basket" of identical global brands would overstate how expensive Romania is.** It samples exactly the branded, sugary and processed goods where Romanian prices come closest to Western ones. Since August 2025 those goods are taxed at 21% VAT in Romania, against 7% for chocolate spreads in Germany. Also, "same brand" does not mean "same product". The Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC) found that 31% (2018/19) and 24% (2021) of tested branded foods differed in composition behind identical or similar packaging. It found no East–West pattern. [T2] [Law]
- **VAT has to be shown both ways, and with product-specific rates.** Food is taxed at 11% or 21% in Romania, 7% or 19% in Germany, 5%, 18% or 27% in Hungary and 20% in Bulgaria. VAT alone explains 3.7% of a Romania–Germany price gap on staples, but 13.1% on a sugary spread. [Law] [Sec]
- **Shelf price and Loyalty price must never be mixed across countries.** EU price statistics rules say loyalty-card prices should count, weighted by sales, *if the data allow*. Statistical offices without scanner data often leave them out. A Comparable without sales data should show both prices side by side and compare like with like. [T2]
- **Nominal euro comparisons are exchange-rate snapshots. Eurostat's price level indices (PLIs) are the official benchmark, but they have limits.** PLIs are national averages, mostly collected in the capital city, surveyed on a three-year cycle and extrapolated in between, "not intended to rank countries strictly", and they have "no regional breakdown". The index should use them to check its own country gaps, not replace them. [T2]
- **Minutes of work has respected precedents but is very sensitive to the wage chosen.** Precedents are the UBS *Prices and Earnings* report (1971–2018) and the Cologne Institute for Economic Research (IW Köln). Of the four offices, only Hungary's KSH publishes a monthly *net median* wage (full-time employees). Romania's INS publishes mean gross and net wages (monthly nationally, annually by county), with no median. Bulgaria's NSI publishes only a quarterly *mean gross* wage. Germany's Destatis publishes an annual *gross median* (full-time) and no net median. The closest like-for-like measures across countries are Eurostat's net earnings for an average worker and the EU income survey's (EU-SILC) median disposable income. [T2]
- **Regional comparisons inside Romania can only be partly truthful.** No official regional price levels exist. County mean net wages range from 4,066 lei (Teleorman) to 7,150 lei (Bucharest) in 2025. In rural households, 7.3% of total spending is the value of food and goods they produce themselves. [T2]
- **Cross-country comparisons by the press have been criticised for checkable errors.** These include price per jar confused with price per kilo, pack sizes not normalised, one store standing for a whole country, regular prices mixed with app prices, and recipes that differ between countries. Academic work finds the *same product in the same chain* differs on average by about 21% across a national border, and the gaps are not persistent. A single snapshot can therefore mislead. [T1] [T4]
- **The closest precedent points the opposite way to Eurostat.** IELKA, a Greek retail research institute, runs a recurring basket comparison of Greece against seven or eight countries, including Romania. It found Romania 5% *dearer* than Greece (December 2024 prices) and 16% dearer (August 2026). Eurostat puts Romanian food about 25% *cheaper* than Greek food. IELKA priced only Carrefour, Auchan and Mega Image in Romania, without Lidl, Kaufland, Profi or Penny. It does not disclose how it matched products, made no wage or purchasing-power adjustment, and its 2026 edition lists a Romanian 5% food VAT rate that was repealed in 2025. Chain selection and data sourcing can reverse the result. [retail-institute study, cross-checked against T2]

## Detailed findings

### 1. What a Basket can and cannot measure

- **Food is a much bigger share of spending in Romania.** In Eurostat's 2025 national-accounts data, food and non-alcoholic beverages were €2,774 per person in Romania against €12,235 of total household consumption (22.7%). Germany: €3,007 of €27,605 (10.9%). Bulgaria: 20.9%. Hungary: 17.3%. These shares are calculated from the nominal-expenditure-per-inhabitant series in [Eurostat prc_ppp_ind_1](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/prc_ppp_ind_1/default/table?lang=en), released 2026-07-09. [T2]
- **Romania's own household survey gives a higher share.** INS's household budget survey for Q2 2026 (released 2026-10-07) says: "produsele agroalimentare și băuturile nealcoolice în valoare de 1581 lei pe o gospodărie au deținut o pondere însemnată în cheltuielile totale de consum medii lunare ale gospodăriilor (32,0%…)". Translation: "agri-food products and non-alcoholic beverages, worth 1,581 lei per household, held a significant share of average monthly household consumption spending (32.0%)" ([INS, ABF T2 2026](https://insse.ro/cms/sites/default/files/com_presa/com_pdf/abf_tr2r26.pdf)). The survey and the national accounts use different definitions; national accounts, for example, include imputed rent. The Overview's "more than a third of household income" figure matches the survey definition, not the cross-country one. [T2]
- **Consequence.** A grocery Basket is a fair proxy for the *food* part of living costs. It says nothing about housing or energy. In a comparison with Germany those dominate, and Romanian prices there are much lower: the overall household consumption price level was 65.1 in Romania against 108.3 in Germany in 2025 ([Eurostat prc_ppp_ind_1](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/prc_ppp_ind_1/default/table?lang=en)). Bulgaria's statistics office attaches this caveat to the same data: "The price levels compared in this article are not adjusted for differences in income or wage levels" ([NSI Bulgaria, 2026-06-18](https://www.nsi.bg/en/press-release/ravnishta-na-potrebitelski-ceni-v-es-9366)). [T2]

### 2. The "we pay more than Germans" narrative

**What the narrative says.** Romanian media regularly put the same Chain's prices in Romania and Germany side by side. In ActiveNews (2016), "Românii, vaci de muls?" ("Romanians, milk cows?") accused Kaufland of charging double for a jar of pickles ([ActiveNews, 2016-07-29](https://www.activenews.ro/economie-consumator/Romanii-vaci-de-muls-Retailerul-Kaufland-acuzat-ca-in-Romania-a-DUBLAT-pretul-unui-produs-pe-care-il-vinde-si-in-Germania.-VEZI-imaginile-135388)). Newsweek România reprinted the Polish tabloid *Fakt*'s Lidl comparison, adding Romanian prices ([Newsweek România, 2023-11-09](https://newsweek.ro/social/diferente-de-preturi-la-produsele-de-baza-lidl-din-romania-germania-si-polonia-unde-e-mai-ieftin)). ### 3. Dual food quality: same brand, different product

- **The first JRC campaign (2018/19).** The JRC compared 1,380 samples of 128 products from 19 Member States. "The composition of 33 % of the evaluated products was identical". It found "differences in composition but identical or similar front-of-pack … for 9 % and 22 %, respectively". It also said: "This clustering did not reveal any consistent pattern of product differentiation for particular geographical regions" ([JRC117088, Ulberth, 2019-06-25](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC117088)). The products were picked from national complaints, so the sample is not representative of the market. Romania was not among the 19 countries ([JRC131303, p. 4 note 7](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC131303/JRC131303_01.pdf)). [T2]
- **Political reaction to the 2019 results.** The JRC's Commissioner said he was "happy that they found no evidence of an East-West divide" and "worried that they uncovered up to one-third of tested products having different compositions" ([FoodNavigator, 2019-06-24](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2019/06/24/Dual-quality-Results-quash-East-West-divide-debate-yet-reveal-disparities-back-of-pack)). [T4 reporting T2]
- **Sensory follow-up.** Trained panels tested 20 products that differed in composition behind similar packs. For 50% of them, the panels could perceive a difference between versions ([JRC131303 executive summary, citing Ulberth 2021](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC131303/JRC131303_01.pdf)). [T2]
- **The second JRC campaign (2021, published 2023-07-20).** It covered 124 products and 1,757 samples in 26 Member States, now including Romania. Products with a different composition behind an identical front-of-pack fell from 9% to 5.6%, and behind a similar one from 22% to 18.5%. Again, "the analysis did not reveal a pattern of product differentiation across geographical areas". In the company survey, most brand owners with differing products had changed packaging, harmonised recipes, or planned to. Others cited national rules, raw materials or consumer preferences ([JRC131303](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC131303/JRC131303_01.pdf)). [T2]
- **The rule since 2022.** Directive (EU) 2019/2161 added Article 6(2)(c) to the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. Marketing a good as identical to one sold in another Member State when it has "significantly different composition or characteristics" can be misleading unless justified by "legitimate and objective factors". Member States have applied it since 28 May 2022, and national authorities assess cases one by one ([Directive 2019/2161](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/2161/oj); described in [JRC131303, p. 7](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC131303/JRC131303_01.pdf), since EUR-Lex could not be read directly). [Law]
- **Implication for the International basket.** Identical branding does not guarantee an identical product. The JRC method is a usable, cheap test. It compares the ingredient list and nutrition panel: "identical" means an exact match, "similar" means under 10% variation, and anything more is "different" ([JRC131303](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC131303/JRC131303_01.pdf)). The index should store a label photo per country and item, and drop or flag any pair that is not identical or similar. [T2]

Key line of the Romanian text: "Cota redusă de 11% se aplică … alimente, inclusiv băuturi … cu excepția: 1. băuturilor alcoolice; 2. băuturilor nealcoolice care se încadrează la codul NC 2202; 3. alimentelor cu zahăr adăugat, al căror conținut total de zahăr este de minimum 10 g/100 g produs". Translation: "The reduced 11% rate applies to food, including drinks, except: 1. alcoholic drinks; 2. non-alcoholic drinks under code NC 2202; 3. foods with added sugar whose total sugar content is at least 10 g per 100 g of product" ([ANAF](https://static.anaf.ro/static/10/Anaf/legislatie/Cod_fiscal_norme_2023.htm)).

What this means for the comparison:

- **VAT explains part of any price gap, by different amounts per product.** For a staple taxed 11% in Romania and 7% in Germany, VAT accounts for 3.7% of the gap. For a soft drink (21% against 19%) it accounts for 1.7%. For a sugary spread (21% against 7%) it accounts for 13.1%. Against Germany, Hungary's 27% adds 18.7% and Bulgaria's 20% adds 12.1% on such a spread (own arithmetic from the rates above).
- **Show both prices.** The headline should be the price with VAT, because that is what shoppers pay; Eurostat price levels also use prices including tax. Next to it, show an ex-VAT column computed *per item* with that item's rate. A flat-rate deduction would be wrong for Romania and Hungary.
- **Rates change.** Romania changed food VAT in August 2025 and Hungary in January 2026. Each Basket snapshot should store the rate it used.

### 5. Shelf price vs Loyalty price

- **What EU price statistics do.** The HICP Methodological Manual 2024 (HICP is the EU's harmonised consumer price index) says discounts limited to a group "or other types of discounts (e.g. lower prices associated with loyalty cards) should be taken into account if allowed by data characteristics". It adds that scanner data (till records from retailers) capture loyalty prices "in proportion to their sales". "In traditional price collection however, it may not be feasible to estimate the proportion of a specific group of households that benefited from a discounted price and therefore such types of discounts are often ignored by NSIs" ([Eurostat, KS-GQ-24-003, p. 118–119](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-manuals-and-guidelines/w/ks-gq-24-003)). The general rule is that price reductions count when "available to all potential consumers with no special conditions attached" ([Statistics Explained, HICP methodology](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=HICP_methodology)). [T2]
- **The truthful price is a sales-weighted mix of shelf and loyalty prices, which the index cannot observe.** Without retailers' sales data, the honest option is the one already in the Overview: show both prices, and compare shelf with shelf and loyalty with loyalty.
- **Cross-country trap.** Loyalty-price practices differ by country and Chain. Money.ro had to use Lidl leaflets for Germany because Lidl Germany does not publish permanent prices online. It also noted that "several items had permanent reductions introduced in spring 2026" in Germany. It used Romanian prices from the Competition Council's price monitor (Monitorul Prețurilor), while the Hungarian and Bulgarian prices came from their official portals or Lidl's sites ([money.ro](https://www.money.ro/stiri/acelasi-cos-de-la-lidl-in-6-tari-romania-germania-elvetia-oct-2026)). One Basket can easily end up mixing price types across countries. [T4]
- **Gap.** The UK competition authority's 2024 review of grocery loyalty pricing could not be retrieved for this note. See "Gaps".

### 6. PPP vs nominal comparison

- **A nominal comparison converts prices at an exchange rate.** It answers "what would a visitor with euros pay". It moves with currencies: the leu averaged 4.9746 per euro in 2024 and 5.0424 in 2025 ([Eurostat ert_bil_eur_a](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/ert_bil_eur_a/default/table?lang=en)). Bulgaria publishes wages in euro in 2026 ([NSI Q2 2026](https://www.nsi.bg/en/file/37294/employees_and_average_wages_and_salaries.pdf)), so it no longer has a currency effect against Germany. [T2]
- **PPP and PLI comparisons are the official tool.** A purchasing power parity (PPP) is the rate that makes the same goods cost the same in two countries; a price level index (PLI) is the PPP divided by the exchange rate. Eurostat lists their limits in its own metadata ([prc_ppp ESMS](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/prc_ppp_esms.htm)): [T2]
  - "In most countries, prices are collected only in the capital city and its surroundings". National averages are then estimated with spatial adjustment factors.
  - "To reduce the response burden, prices are collected over a period of three years"; years without a survey are extrapolated. The 2025 figures use prices "collected in 2023 and 2024 … extrapolated to 2025 using detailed consumer price indices" ([Statistics Explained](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Comparative_price_levels_of_consumer_goods_and_services)).
  - "[PPPs] are not intended to establish a strict ranking of countries", and "There is no regional breakdown".
  - Products are chosen to be "comparable and representative". Leaving out how representative a product is "may produce a bias in the results".
- **Revisions are material.** Romania's 2024 food price level was 75.5 in the July 2025 release (COICOP 1999 basis) and 77.9 in the July 2026 release (COICOP 2018 basis) ([prc_ppp_ind](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/prc_ppp_ind/default/table?lang=en) vs [prc_ppp_ind_1](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/prc_ppp_ind_1/default/table?lang=en)). A gap of a few points between countries is within the noise. [T2]
- **What this means for the index.** Eurostat's categories are built to be *representative* of what each country buys. An identical-brand Basket is the opposite: highly *comparable* but unrepresentative. Both can be shown, as long as each is labelled for what it is. The country view should display the index's own price gap next to Eurostat's food price level for the same countries and explain any divergence.

### 7. Minutes of work

**Precedents.**

- **UBS *Prices and Earnings* (1971–2018, every three years).**
  - The 2003 edition compared 70 cities and expressed affordability as "37 minutes' work for a Big Mac". It stated: "Wherever possible, the survey focused on local products and wages for local workers" ([UBS media release, 2003-08-19](https://scarpaz.com/attic/Documents/PuL2003en.pdf), copy hosted by a third party).
  - The 2018 edition used 77 cities and a 128-item basket. Earnings came from "a reference profile of 15 professions". Net wages were gross wages minus taxes and social contributions, divided by annual hours ([Chronicle.lu, 2018-05-29](https://chronicle.lu/category/surveys-reports/25969-luxembourg-rates-highly-in-global-cities-ranking-2018)).
  - 2018 results: a Big Mac took about 55.6 minutes of work in Budapest (65th of 77), and an iPhone about 395.7 hours in Bucharest ([Xpatloop, 2018-07-23](https://xpatloop.com/channels/2018/07/big-mac-index-reveals-current-purchasing-power-in-hungary.html)).
  - UBS's minisite was indexed as saying UBS "no longer produces the Prices & Earnings report", because newer comparisons "made the UBS study obsolete" ([UBS minisite](https://www.ubs.com/minisites/prices-earnings/); the page returned HTTP 404 when fetched on 2026-10-21). [T2-like private research, reported via T4]
- **IW Köln, "Kaufkraft pro Lohnminute" (purchasing power per minute of wage).** It uses average *net hourly* earnings in Germany. Its researcher said: "Seit der deutschen Einheit bis 2021 ist die Kaufkraft pro Lohnminute um 27 Prozent gestiegen". Translation: "Since German reunification until 2021, purchasing power per wage-minute has risen 27%" ([Tageskarte reporting IW, 2022-08-10](https://www.tageskarte.io/zahlen/detail/kaufkraft-pro-lohnminute-drei-minuten-arbeit-fuer-einen-liter-vollmilch.html)). [T4 reporting research]
- **Money.ro's "Baskets per wage" (2026).** It used Eurostat's net pay for a single person without children on the national average wage ([money.ro](https://www.money.ro/stiri/acelasi-cos-de-la-lidl-in-6-tari-romania-germania-elvetia-oct-2026)). [T4]

**Why the measure is fragile.**

- **The wage measure changes the answer by about a third.** Section 2 gives 2.05× (single average worker, net) against 2.75× (median household disposable income) for Romania vs Germany, from the same prices.
- **Mean against median.** In Eurostat's 2022 earnings survey, the median monthly gross wage was 75.3% of the mean in Romania, 73.3% in Bulgaria, 79.7% in Hungary and 86.1% in Germany. Romania: median 4,675 lei against mean 6,210 lei ([earn_ses22_mdeci](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/earn_ses22_mdeci/default/table?lang=en), [earn_ses22_20](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/earn_ses22_20/default/table?lang=en)). Using the national mean flatters Romanian purchasing power against Germany by about 14%. [T2]
- **Hours.** Minutes of work need an *hourly* wage. INS publishes a mean net hourly wage (40.31 lei in August 2026, TEMPO table FOM109C, updated 2026-10-13) and Destatis a mean gross hourly wage (€28.19, April 2025, [Destatis](https://www.destatis.de/DE/Themen/Arbeit/Verdienste/Verdienste-Branche-Berufe/_inhalt.html)). Neither is a median, and they are not comparable with each other. [T2]
- **Who earns.** A wage view says nothing about pensioners, the unemployed or the self-employed, which matters for the Overview's Pensioner Basket.

The INS TEMPO catalogue lists 1,930 tables. A search of their titles for "median" found no earnings median, only median age and income-poverty thresholds (catalogue retrieved 2026-10-21).

The methodology says the median "a teljes munkaidőben alkalmazásban állókra vonatkozik" ("refers to full-time employees"). **Conclusion.** Only Hungary meets the Overview's "median net wage from the national statistics office" literally. For Romania, Bulgaria and Germany the nearest national figure is a mean (INS, NSI) or a gross median (Destatis). A cross-country view that mixes them would compare different things. Use one harmonised Eurostat measure for country comparisons, and national figures only within a country.

### 9. How Comparables' and media comparisons have been criticised

- **Unit price errors.** In the 2016 Kaufland pickles case, readers showed that 14.99 lei was the price per kilo of *drained* weight, not per jar. Per kilo, the price was about the same in both countries. Kaufland Romania replied that prices depend on logistics and small import volumes: "Un produs este întotdeauna mai ieftin în țara în care este făcut". Translation: "A product is always cheaper in the country where it is made" ([ActiveNews](https://www.activenews.ro/economie-consumator/Romanii-vaci-de-muls-Retailerul-Kaufland-acuzat-ca-in-Romania-a-DUBLAT-pretul-unui-produs-pe-care-il-vinde-si-in-Germania.-VEZI-imaginile-135388)). [T4]
- **Unnormalised sizes, one store, no date.** The *Fakt*/Newsweek list compares Gouda in 150–200 g packs and sausages in 350–400 g packs. Its German prices come from a single Düsseldorf store, and no collection date or promotion status is given. Its text contradicts its own table on sugar ([Newsweek România](https://newsweek.ro/social/diferente-de-preturi-la-produsele-de-baza-lidl-din-romania-germania-si-polonia-unde-e-mai-ieftin)). [T4]
- **Mixed sources and recipes.** Money.ro's careful 2026 comparison still had to convert pack sizes (German sugar from 2 kg, Coca-Cola from 1.25 l), leave out deposit packaging in Hungary, and mix leaflets with portals. It warns that "brands and recipes may differ between countries" ([money.ro](https://www.money.ro/stiri/acelasi-cos-de-la-lidl-in-6-tari-romania-germania-elvetia-oct-2026)). [T4]
- **Snapshots are unstable.** Using transaction data along the Austria–Germany border, ECB economists found that "Even within multinational retail chains the prices of identical products on the two sides of the border differ on average by about 21%", and that "These price differences are not very persistent" ([Messner, Rumler & Strasser, ECB WP 2776, Feb 2023](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecb.wp2776~ed7ac4620d.en.pdf)). One week's gap for one product is weak evidence of a structural gap. [T1]
- **Structural explanations are contested.** Retailers in some markets blame "territorial supply constraints": suppliers restricting sales across borders. A European Parliament Research Service briefing ([EPRS, 2025-05-07](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/thinktank/en/document/EPRS_BRI(2025)772850)) repeats a 2020 Commission study's estimate of "around €14 billion" in consumer savings if they were removed. Wikipedia's summary of that study ([VVA 2020](https://data.europa.eu/doi/10.2873/59256)) puts a 90% range of €0.5–28 billion on the estimate. An industry-commissioned review argues the same price patterns appear for retailers' own private labels ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_supply_constraints); underlying documents not read). Neither side should be presented as settled. [T2, contested]
- **Statistical offices' own warnings.** Eurostat says price level indices are "not intended to rank countries strictly" ([Statistics Explained](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Comparative_price_levels_for_food,_beverages_and_tobacco)). Bulgaria's statistics office warns its price levels are "not adjusted for differences in income or wage levels" ([NSI Bulgaria](https://www.nsi.bg/en/press-release/ravnishta-na-potrebitelski-ceni-v-es-9366)). [T2]
- **No formal evaluation found.** We found no academic or regulatory evaluation of a *Comparable's* cross-country comparison. The criticism above is of media comparisons, some built on Comparables' data (Monitorul Prețurilor, the Hungarian competition authority's (GVH) price monitor, Bulgaria's official portal). See "Gaps".

#### Case study: IELKA's international basket comparison (Greece vs Europe, including Romania)

IELKA (Ινστιτούτο Έρευνας Λιανικής Καταναλωτικών Αγαθών, the Institute for Retail Consumer Goods Research) describes itself as "Ν.Π.Ι.Δ. μη κερδοσκοπικού χαρακτήρα με κοινωφελή ερευνητικό σκοπό" ("a non-profit private-law entity with a public-benefit research purpose"). Its scientific committee is mostly made up of Athens University of Economics and Business academics. It says it "does not represent its members before any authority". The pages we read did not list its members or funders ([About](https://ielka.gr/%cf%84%ce%b1%cf%85%cf%84%cf%8c%cf%84%ce%b7%cf%84%ce%b1-%cf%80%cf%81%ce%b1%ce%ba%cf%84%ce%b9%ce%ba%ce%ad%cf%82/), [Organisation](https://ielka.gr/%ce%b5%cf%80%ce%b9%cf%83%cf%84%ce%b7%ce%bc%ce%bf%ce%bd%ce%b9%ce%ba%ce%ae-%ce%b5%cf%80%ce%b9%cf%84%cf%81%ce%bf%cf%80%ce%ae/)). The coordinator's lead says Economedia reported the February 2025 edition in Romania; that article was not read for this note.

**Method, as published.**

- **Editions.** The comparison is repeated: 20 May 2024 and 16 September 2024 (each with Romania), 24 February 2025 (December 2024 prices), and 24 September 2026 (August 2026 prices, Bulgaria added) ([announcements archive](https://ielka.gr/category/%ce%b1%ce%bd%ce%b1%ce%ba%ce%bf%ce%b9%ce%bd%cf%8e%cf%83%ce%b5%ce%b9%cf%82/page/4/)).
- **Scale.** The February 2025 edition covers 44 supermarket chains, 41 product categories and "over 6,000 prices" ([IELKA, 2025-02-24](https://ielka.gr/sygkrisi-timwn-12-2024/)).
- **Products.** It uses "τελικές τιμές τόσο επώνυμων προϊόντων, όσο και προϊόντα ιδιωτικής ετικέτας" ("final prices of both branded and private-label products") ([IELKA, 2026-09-24](https://ielka.gr/anakoinosi-24-09-2026/)).
- **Sources.** Foreign prices come from price comparison platforms and price observatories. Greek prices come from price collection at Greek chains.
- **Romanian Chains.** Only Carrefour, Auchan and Mega Image. In Germany: Edeka, Rewe, Kaufland, Lekkerland, Bela and Norma, without Aldi or Lidl.
- **VAT.** Results are shown "με και χωρίς την αξία του ΦΠΑ, ο οποίος διαφέρει σε κάθε χώρα" ("with and without VAT, which differs in each country") ([2026 edition](https://ielka.gr/anakoinosi-24-09-2026/)).
- **Taxes not covered.** The authors note that excise duties (for example on coffee) "cannot be taken into account".
- **Wages.** Wage and labour costs are listed among about 15 factors affecting prices, but no wage or purchasing-power adjustment is made.
- **Not disclosed.** How products were matched across countries (identical or equivalent), how differences in composition were handled, and how loyalty prices or promotions were treated.

**Results for Romania**, with Greece as the base:

| Edition | Reference prices | Romania vs Greece, with VAT | Without VAT | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 24 Feb 2025 | Dec 2024 | +5% | +10% | [IELKA](https://ielka.gr/sygkrisi-timwn-12-2024/) |
| 24 Sep 2026 | Aug 2026 | +16% | +22% | [IELKA](https://ielka.gr/anakoinosi-24-09-2026/) |

**Cross-check against official data (own analysis).**

1. **Eurostat says the opposite.** Its price levels for food and non-alcoholic beverages (EU = 100) are Romania 77.9 against Greece 106.2 for 2024, and 79.8 against 105.3 for 2025 ([prc_ppp_ind_1](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/prc_ppp_ind_1/default/table?lang=en)). Romanian food is therefore about 25% *cheaper* than Greek food, not 5–16% dearer. [T2]
2. **Chain selection.** Leaving out the discounters that lead the Romanian market, and Aldi and Lidl in Germany, while using in-store collection in Greece, can plausibly explain much of the reversal. This cannot be tested without IELKA's product-level data.
3. **VAT rates.** The 2026 edition lists Romania's food VAT as "5% or 11%". Romania's 5% rate was repealed: Art. 291(3) of the Fiscal Code reads "ABROGAT" in [ANAF's consolidated text](https://static.anaf.ro/static/10/Anaf/legislatie/Cod_fiscal_norme_2023.htm). Sugary foods and soft drinks are taxed at 21%. Ex-VAT figures for Romania may therefore be mis-stated. The February 2025 edition's 9% was correct for December 2024. [Law]
4. **Disclosure.** A retail institute's comparison that always concludes its home country is cheaper ("Η τάση αυτή … είναι διαχρονική την τελευταία 12ετία", "this trend … has held for the last 12 years") should disclose its funders and full data to be citable. We found no published product-level dataset.

**Lessons for our index.** IELKA shows good practice in some respects: showing prices with and without VAT, naming the chains, giving the sample size and repeating the study. It also shows the main ways a cross-country Basket goes wrong: unrepresentative Chain selection, different data sources per country, undisclosed product matching, out-of-date tax rates, and no benchmark against Eurostat.

### 10. Regions inside Romania

- **No official regional prices.** INS publishes the consumer price index only nationally. Its average-price tables cover agricultural and market prices, not supermarket Baskets (TEMPO catalogue: PPA101A–PPA103A, IPC series). Eurostat price levels have "no regional breakdown" ([ESMS](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/prc_ppp_esms.htm)). [T2]
- **Regional wage data exist only as county means.** In 2025, mean net earnings were 1.76 times higher in Bucharest (7,150 lei) than in Teleorman (4,066 lei) (INS TEMPO FOM106H, updated 2026-09-18). INS adds that annual county figures include back-pay and compensation sums that the monthly series excludes. [T2]
- **Own production matters.** In Q2 2026, food and goods produced by households themselves were worth 4.3% of total household spending: 2.4% in towns and 7.3% in villages ([INS ABF](https://insse.ro/cms/sites/default/files/com_presa/com_pdf/abf_tr2r26.pdf)). A supermarket Basket overstates what rural households actually pay for food. [T2]
- **What a regional comparison can say.** It can be truthful about the *same Chain's prices in two cities*, and about *work time at the county mean wage*. It cannot speak to regional cost of living without regional rent and price data. If a Chain's online prices are national, the "regional" price difference is zero by construction. The Overview leaves store-level pricing out of scope, so this needs checking per Chain.

## What a truthful comparison would need

1. **An honest name.** "Grocery Basket price" and "grocery Basket affordability (minutes of work)", never "cost of living". Show food's share of household spending next to every country comparison.
2. **An official benchmark.** Show Eurostat's food price level for each country next to the index's own gap, and explain divergences.
3. **A representative Basket per country.** Keep the identical-brand comparison only as a labelled "branded goods" view. Check composition per country with the JRC method (ingredient list and nutrition panel within 10%), and publish label photos.
4. **Strict like-for-like rules.** Unit prices per kg or litre within a stated pack-size band, drained weight for preserves, deposits shown separately, and the same price type (shelf or loyalty) on both sides.
5. **Prices averaged over several weeks** before claiming a cross-country gap, given how quickly gaps change.
6. **VAT shown both ways.** Price with VAT as the headline, plus an ex-VAT column using each item's rate, with the rates and their effective dates stored in each snapshot.
7. **Stated exchange rates.** The ECB reference rate on the collection date, stated, plus a PPP-adjusted line.
8. **One harmonised wage source per view, named exactly.** For example, "Eurostat net earnings of a single worker on the average wage, 2025". Show the sensitivity to the wage measure (mean vs median, individual vs household), and use national medians only for within-country views (KSH for Hungary). For Pensioner Baskets, use pensions, not wages.
9. **Regional claims limited to the same Chain's prices and county mean wages**, with the own-production caveat.
10. **Full transparency.** Publish raw prices, dates, sources, rates and corrections, and offer Chains a documented right of reply before publication of a cross-country claim.

## Gaps and uncertainties

- **Web search ran out partway through this research.** The shared session quota was exhausted, so the following were *not* verified:
  - Romania's transposition and enforcement of UCPD Art. 6(2)(c), and any consumer-protection authority (ANPC) tests on dual quality.
  - The UK competition authority's 2024 loyalty-pricing review.
  - Current status of retail margin caps in Romania and Hungary, which would distort cross-country price gaps.
  - Bulgaria's district-level wage tables and Hungary's county-level medians.
  - Any formal criticism of the Hungarian (GVH) or Bulgarian state price portals' cross-country use.
  - How IELKA's comparison was received in Romania (the February 2025 Economedia report), and who funds IELKA.
  A follow-up session can close these.
- **Eurostat 2025 price levels are extrapolations** of 2023–2024 surveys, collected mostly in capital cities, and revised by up to about 2.4 points between releases.
- **EU-SILC 2025 refers to 2024 incomes.** Eurostat's average-worker net earnings are model calculations, not observed pay.
- **National-accounts food spending includes own-account production.** Romania's real food volume per person (122, EU = 100) is high and should not be read as Romanians "eating more" without checking definitions.
- **Statistical secrecy and coverage differ:** KSH uses full administrative data, NSI a sample survey, Destatis a survey of employee records, and INS enterprise surveys.
- **The UBS minisite quote** comes from a search index; the live page returned 404.
- **The territorial supply constraint evidence is contested**, and the underlying 2020 study and industry critique were not read directly.

## Questions this raises

- **Should the International basket survive the MVP re-scope?** It would become a labelled "branded goods" view, or it could be replaced by Eurostat price levels plus a small, composition-checked branded subset. This feeds the map's "Re-scoping the MVP" item.
- **Which wage measure is the headline for minutes of work?** A harmonised Eurostat measure (comparable but model-based and annual) or national figures (timelier but not comparable)? Do we show a range rather than one number?
- **What does the Pensioner Basket use instead of wages?** Minutes of *pension* at the INS average pension?
- **Is own price collection in Germany, Hungary and Bulgaria feasible and lawful?** Lidl Germany does not publish permanent prices online, and logged-in app prices raise the terms-of-service issues already noted in the Overview. This should be added to the legal tickets' scope.
- **Should the index publish a right-of-reply process for Chains** before any cross-country claim, given how often such claims have been wrong in the press?
- **Could INS, or Romania's Competition Council through Monitorul Prețurilor, be asked for regional price data** or a median wage series? Romania's Structure of Earnings Survey already measures a median every four years.
